Worked examples

Study a complete solution with reasons before attempting a related problem.

Introductory · 3 drills · Reviewed 2026-10-24

See an examplePractise

What it helps you do

Give a novice a visible procedure and rationale rather than asking them to discover every step unaided.

Starting knowledge: No specialist knowledge is required.

  • Show the starting information, goal, and complete sequence.
  • Explain why each step follows.
  • Follow the example with a related problem that requires the learner to act.

The problem it came from

Worked examples have a long educational history. Sweller and Cooper’s 1985 algebra study is an influential primary investigation of learning from supplied solutions.

The teaching choice is how much of the process to show and when to fade support. Results from a particular learning task do not prove that examples always outperform independent practice.

Work through an example

STARTING POINT
Find 11 in the sorted list [2, 5, 8, 11, 14]. Answer: position 4.
APPLYING THE APPROACH
1. Inspect the middle item: 8.
2. Since 11 is greater than 8, discard the lower half including 8.
3. Inspect the remaining middle candidate: 11.
4. Report its position as 4 when counting from 1.
  • The explanation preserves the sorted-list prerequisite.
  • The discarded region follows a comparison, not a guess.
  • A different example is needed to practise an absent target.

Where it helps. Where it stops.

Use it when

  • Introducing an unfamiliar calculation, algorithm, or document technique.
  • A learner can imitate an answer but cannot explain the intermediate steps.

Potential benefits

  • An annotated solution makes usually hidden transitions inspectable.
  • A follow-up problem can expose whether the learner transferred the method.

Limits and common mistakes

  • Copying a solution can feel like understanding without producing it.
  • An expert may need less guidance; unnecessary detail can get in their way.
